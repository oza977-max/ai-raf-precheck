1 · Executive Summaryv1.0 · Restricted

1. Executive Summary

Regulators across every jurisdiction relevant to a global financial institution — MAS, FCA/PRA, BaFin, FSB, FSA Japan, and the US agencies under SR 26-2 — have moved from principles to enforceable, and in places divergent, expectations on AI risk. The convergent message remains: AI risk is not a standalone governance exercise but a set of risk dimensions integrated into the existing Risk Appetite Framework, calibrated against use-case materiality, and owned at Board level.

The most significant feature of the current regulatory landscape is divergence. In April 2026 the US agencies issued SR 26-2, superseding SR 11-7 and SR 21-8 and explicitly excluding generative and agentic AI from model risk scope pending a separate Request for Information (RFI). The UK PRA (SS1/23) takes the opposite, technology-agnostic position; Canada's OSFI E-23 is broader still. A global firm can no longer rely on a single definition of "model." This framework therefore adopts a triple-track classification that is jurisdiction-aware by design.

This supplement maps AI-specific tolerances to the firm's existing Operational Risk, Model Risk, Technology Risk, and Conduct Risk categories. The Appetite Register (§6) is binding; §7–§12 cover agentic AI, verification, incident response, governance, and implementation. Appetite is differentiated by a four-tier materiality model (Critical / High / Medium / Low) using Impact-dominant, rules-based assignment.

How to use this template

  • All [FIRM] placeholders must be replaced with the adopting organisation's name, entity references, and legal structure.
  • Dashed overlay boxes identify content that must be completed by the firm — existing policy cross-references, committee names, live inventory data, and calibrated KRI thresholds.
  • The Appetite Register (§6) contains representative use cases only; replace with the firm's actual AI inventory.
  • Jurisdiction coverage in §3 is illustrative for a global financial institution; trim to the regimes applicable to the adopting firm.

5 · Classification & Tieringv1.0 · Restricted

5. Classification & Materiality Tiering

Triple-track classification

Every AI system is assigned to one of three governance tracks. Traditional ML is universally in model risk scope; the contested category is generative and agentic AI, which splits by use.

TrackPopulationRegime
I — Traditional MRMStatistical/quantitative models: regression, GBM, scorecards, VaR, IRBFull model validation, monitoring, conceptual soundness — all jurisdictions
II — AI on MRMGenAI/agentic producing a quantitative output for a material decision, or replacing a Track I systemFull MRM plus AI-specific controls (grounding, drift, fingerprinting)
III — AI GovernanceGenAI/agentic that informs but does not decide — narrative, productivity, augmentationInventory, tier, HITL, sampling, perimeter — documented, lighter than full MRM. Where the governing jurisdiction applies a technology-agnostic MRM standard (e.g. UK SS1/23), Track III is supplemented with that standard's obligations; track assignment never reduces the jurisdictional minimum (see §3 operating rule).

Classification test, applied in order: (0) autonomy Level 4 on irreversible, client- or market-facing actions → outside appetite, do not classify (see §7); (1) traditional quantitative model → Track I; (2) quantitative output into a regulated decision → Track II; (3) replaces a prior model → Track II; (4) autonomy Level 3+, or human override rate <5% at registration → Track II. All "no" → Track III. Annual re-classification; trigger-based on autonomy change, use-case expansion, or jurisdictional update.

Materiality tiering — Impact-dominant

Tier is assigned by rules, not multiplication. Impact dominates: a high-impact use case cannot be down-tiered by low complexity or low reliance.

TierTrigger (Impact-dominant)ApprovalValidation
CriticalRegulated decision, capital, client financial outcome, or external-facing at scaleCRO + Board committeeFull validation + ongoing monitoring
HighMaterial internal decision, risk measurement input, or confidential data at scaleAI Governance Cttee + Model RiskIndependent 2LoD validation
MediumAnalytical support, client-adjacent, limited blast radius2LoD reviewDocumented design review + sampling
LowInternal productivity, no regulated decision, low data sensitivityFast Track1LoD self-attestation
EU AI Act override. Any use case classified as high-risk under EU AI Act Annex III (e.g. credit scoring, employment) is assigned Critical regardless of the internal tiering outcome.

Fast Track (Low tier) relies on 1LoD self-attestation, but the Low-tier population is subject to periodic 2LoD sampling and audit; registering a use case as Low to avoid oversight is treated as a conduct matter, not a classification choice.

7 · Agentic AIv1.0 · Restricted

7. Agentic AI — Specific Appetite

Agentic systems act rather than merely predict. No regulator has issued specific guidance, and existing MRM was designed for predictive systems. The firm therefore applies a precautionary, autonomy-graded appetite.

Autonomy levelDefinitionAppetite
Level 0Human decides; AI provides data onlyAccept
Level 1AI recommends; human approves each actionAccept
Level 2AI acts; human monitors in real timeTolerate
Level 3AI acts; human audits ex-postTolerate (bounded, reversible only)
Level 4Fully autonomous — outside appetite for irreversible, client- or market-facing actions; bounded, fully-reversible autonomy is governed as Level 3Do not accept
Hard constraints for all agentic systems. No agent may exceed its assigned task, acquire permissions beyond it, loop indefinitely, or take an irreversible action without human approval. In implementation: minimal-permission default (read before write, write before execute); action-level approval gates by consequence; recursion and loop limits; complete tool-call logging; kill switch and graceful degradation by design. Irreversible actions require Level 1 or below regardless of tier.

9 · Verification & Assurancev1.0 · Restricted

9. Verification & Assurance

Classical model validation assumes deterministic output checkable against ground truth. Generative AI defies this — output is text with no obvious back-test. This is the central reason SR 26-2 excluded GenAI from MRM. The framework addresses verification explicitly rather than treating it as solved.

Three verification modes

ModeApplies toMethod
GroundingRAG, synthesis, summarisationEvery claim must resolve to a retrievable source; citation-confidence scoring; failed grounding falls back to human review
BehaviouralRecommendation, classification, routingContinuous sampling against human ground truth; disagreement triage; concept-drift detection
AdversarialAll systems, periodicStructured red-teaming: injection, jailbreak, exfil, role confusion; quarterly minimum, pre-launch for Track II

Grounding verification — implementation requirements

For RAG, synthesis, and summarisation systems, the grounding control requires a structured, claim-level verification process. The firm's implementation must satisfy the following minimum requirements.

RequirementStandardFailure disposition
Claim-level resolutionEach factual or analytical assertion in the output is matched to a specific, retrievable source passage; match must be semantically sufficient, not merely lexically proximateUnresolved claim → flagged; output returned to human review or suppressed before use in a decision
Confidence scoringResolution confidence is assessed per claim: High (direct support), Medium (inferential), Low (weak or indirect). Aggregate score determines escalationAggregate below firm-defined threshold → escalate to human before output is used
Citation attachmentVerified claims carry explicit citations in output (source ID, chunk reference, confidence tier); unverified output is marked as suchOutput presented to end-user without citations is a grounding control failure — incident-class event under §10
Audit loggingResolution chain is logged per output: claim, source, confidence, timestamp, model version. Log is immutable and available to 2LoD on demandAbsent log treated as unverifiable assurance; recorded as control gap at next validation cycle
Grounding verification and Track II validation. For Track II systems, grounding logs form part of the validation evidence package. A system that resolves claims correctly against its corpus but whose corpus is itself incomplete or stale fails on coverage grounds — grounding verification tests resolution fidelity, not source completeness; the latter is a separate validation obligation.
Silent substitution. Hosted models are updated by vendors without notice, invalidating prior validation overnight. Output fingerprinting against a fixed probe set detects this. A step-change in aggregate grounding confidence scores against a stable corpus is also a behavioural indicator of silent substitution and triggers a fingerprinting check. Zero tolerance for unmonitored model change on Track II systems.

Verification is distinct from monitoring. Monitoring asks whether behaviour is consistent with the past; verification asks whether output is true and grounded. A model can pass consistency checks while drifting from ground truth, and can be consistent yet consistently wrong. Both are required; neither substitutes for the other.

Firm overlay requiredSpecify the firm's chosen tooling or methodology for grounding verification and name it here; define the confidence threshold that triggers human escalation; confirm audit log retention period and access controls.

Model approval and provenance

Verification tells you whether a given output can be trusted. It says nothing about whether the model producing it was ever cleared to run in the firm at all. The firm maintains an approved-model list, and no AI use case may run on a model absent from it without triggering review.

Provenance classDefinitionApproval implication
Vendor-hostedCalled via a third party's API; the firm does not control weights or hostingSubject to the silent-substitution controls above; vendor concentration tracked per §6
Open-weights, self-hostedWeights obtained from a public release, run on firm-controlled infrastructureNo vendor substitution risk, but no vendor accountability either — the firm carries the full validation burden itself
Fine-tuned in-houseA base model (vendor-hosted or open-weights) further trained on firm dataInherits the base model's provenance class plus training-data governance obligations

Approval requires named benchmark evidence appropriate to the intended use — general-capability benchmarks are not sufficient on their own where the use case is financial; evidence must include a finance-domain evaluation for any Track I/II use and for any Track III use that touches client-facing or regulated content. Provenance class is a firm-visible attribute the appetite rules may condition on; it is never itself a pass/fail gate — an open-weights self-hosted model with strong finance-domain benchmark evidence may sit inside appetite where a vendor-hosted model without evidence does not.

Firm overlay requiredName the owner of the approved-model list (proposed: Model Risk, with AI Governance Committee sign-off); specify the benchmark suites accepted as evidence and their minimum currency (e.g. re-run within 12 months).

Risk-knowledge awareness

Sections A–H and the controls above encode what the firm has already decided to check for. They do not, by construction, cover a risk class no one at the firm has yet written a rule for — that gap is invisible until an incident makes it visible. The firm separately maintains awareness against a recognized external AI-risk taxonomy, reviewed by a named owner whenever the taxonomy is updated, so that gaps become rule-authoring work before they become incidents rather than after.

This awareness is advisory only. It may flag that a use case's shape matches a known risk class and note where the firm's rules are silent on it; it has no authority to accept, reject, tier, or attach a control to a use case. Only Sections B–F, and the jurisdiction packs at §3, decide a verdict. Where an external taxonomy flags a gap, the disposition is always the same: the gap is logged for a human rule-author to consider, never auto-applied.

Firm overlay requiredName the taxonomy adopted (a public option: the MIT AI Risk Repository, CC BY 4.0 licensed); name the owner responsible for reviewing it on each update; set the review cadence.

11. Governance and Three Lines of Defence

LineRole for AIKey responsibilities

Internal Audit		Independent assuranceAudit the framework's operation; specifically test 1LoD/2LoD separation in shared AI infrastructure
On the dual-mandate conflict. Where a firm operates both "AI in Risk" (build) and "AI Risk" (govern), these must sit in separate reporting lines within Risk, with 1LoD-equivalent build teams distinct from 2LoD governance. 3LoD audit is specifically scoped to test that this separation holds in practice — the control is structural, and is verified, not assumed.
Multi-platform concentration. Where the firm operates AI systems across more than one cloud or model provider, third-party concentration appetite applies per provider, with tighter limits where the firm is a deployer rather than developer.
2LoD resourcing. The 2LoD mandate above — classify and tier every system, validate Track I/II, set and monitor KRIs, approve Medium+ use cases, run red-teams — presumes adequate, ring-fenced headcount and validation capability. The framework is only as strong as the second line that operates it; under-resourcing it is the most likely cause of failure.

12. Implementation Roadmap

PhaseWindowObservable when complete
1 — Foundation0–12 moRAF supplement approved; inventory non-empty & growing; three-zone perimeter enforced; triple-track classification applied at registration; Fast Track operational; EU AI Act Art. 50(2) transparency addressed for applicable systems
2 — Integration12–24 moStandard Track live; Zone B in production; HITL protocols documented with measured override rates; KRIs in quarterly reporting; first read-only agents
3 — Augmentation24–36 moLevel 3 autonomy for a bounded use case with ex-post audit; Zone C on-prem live; verification automated; vendor concentration tracked

The maturity curve is a sequencing principle, not a timeline. Phase 1 cannot be skipped without paying the retrofitting cost later; Phase 4 cannot be rushed — the capability and governance judgement it requires accumulate only through operating experience.
