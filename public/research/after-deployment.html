Part one

What the evidence base actually says

The MIT AI Risk Repository (Slattery et al., MIT FutureTech) is the largest structured catalogue of AI risks in the academic literature: 1,724 individual risks extracted from 80 frameworks and papers, each coded for what caused it, whether it was intentional, and — the axis this briefing is about — when it occurs. We analysed the V4 release (December 2025) directly for this briefing; every number in this section is computed from the source file, not quoted from a summary.

Of the risks that could be coded for timing, 977 occur post-deployment against 190 pre-deployment — 84% of the codable set. (A further 430 entries are timing-ambiguous or span both phases; even counting all of those against, post-deployment risks are still an outright majority of everything coded.) And the skew is not an artefact of older literature: papers published in 2025 alone code 88% of their risks as post-deployment — the newest research leans harder into the pattern, not softer.

The governance consequence is direct: misuse (280 risks) and malfunction (225 risks) are the two dominant quadrants, and no single control covers both. Adversarial testing never catches quiet drift; drift monitoring never catches a fraudster. A monitoring regime is only credible if it runs both.

The live incident trackers agree with the catalogue. The AI Incident Database's own Q2 2026 roundup logged 148 new incidents in three months (May–July 2026), with AI-enabled scams and fraud its single largest editorial cluster (27 incidents) — the same adversarial cluster that tops Figure 3. The OECD's AI Incidents Monitor now screens over 150,000 news articles daily to surface roughly ten confirmed incidents or hazards a day. And Stanford's Index adds the recurrence finding: the share of organisations reporting one or two incidents fell (42%→29%) while the share reporting three to five rose (30%→50%) — incidents are not spread thinly across the market; they compound inside the firms that deploy most.

Part two

Why post-deployment dominates — and why it is getting worse

Three mechanisms explain the skew (§1, §2, §4), and a compounding pressure amplifies all of them (§3) — each with hard 2025–26 measurements behind it.

1. The model you approved is not the model you are running

Hosted LLMs change under their consumers without version bumps. The canonical measurement (Chen, Zaharia & Zou, published in Harvard Data Science Review): GPT-4's directly-executable code generation fell from over 50% to 10% in three months (March–June 2023) with no version change, and its accuracy on a simple number-theory task fell from 84% to 51% over the same window. The mechanism is still live: Anthropic's September 2025 postmortem documents a routing bug that at its worst hour hit 16% of Claude Sonnet 4 requests, with roughly 30% of Claude Code users catching at least one degraded response — weeks of production impact, no API change.

For a bank this means the system that passed validation in March is behaviourally a different system in June — a risk class ("silent model substitution") that by definition cannot exist pre-deployment.

2. Adversaries only show up after launch

The three largest post-deployment clusters in Figure 3 — disinformation at scale, fraud and manipulation, cyberattack enablement — all require a deployed system to exploit or to weaponise. The attacker's own capability curve is the alarming one: on Stanford's Cybench benchmark of realistic cybersecurity challenges, AI agents' unguided solve rate went from 15% to 93% in a single year (2024→2025).

3. The compounding pressure: more deployment, less visibility

Stanford's AI Index reports 362 documented incidents in 2025, up 55% year-on-year, with organisational AI adoption at 88% — and its Foundation Model Transparency Index fell from 58 to 40 over the same period. McKinsey's global survey reports 51% of AI-using organisations with at least one negative consequence already — and the firms using AI most reporting more consequences, not fewer, despite broader risk programmes. More deployment, more incidents, less visibility into the models underneath — three trends moving the wrong way at once. They come from unrelated surveys and prove no common cause; a bank sits at their intersection either way.

4. Autonomy moves the failure point downstream

Agentic systems act rather than draft, which converts every model error from "a person caught it in review" into "a transaction happened." The reliability numbers are unforgiving: on τ-bench, a controlled benchmark of AI agents handling realistic customer-service tasks with real tools, a frontier model completed 61% of retail-domain tasks on a first attempt (and only about 35% on the harder airline domain) — but completed all eight repeats of the same task less than 25% of the time. Consistency, not capability, is the production gap.

Gartner predicts over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end-2027 on cost, value and inadequate risk controls; the Bank of England warned in June 2026 that existing oversight frameworks were not designed for autonomous agents in payments and trading. The MIT data agrees from the other direction: human–computer interaction risks (overreliance, ceded agency) are 100% post-deployment.

A pre-approval gate answers "should this ship?" The evidence says the harder question is "is this still the thing we approved, and is it still behaving?" — asked continuously, for the life of the system.

Part three

The financial-industry overlay

Banking concentrates the conditions under which post-deployment risk does the most damage: irreversible transactions, regulated fairness duties, market-correlated behaviour, and deep third-party dependence. The supervisors' own survey data now quantifies all four — and the concentration numbers are the starkest.

The same survey's cloud-provider concentration is starker still — the top three hold roughly three-quarters of UK financial-services cloud usage. Figure 4 shows the model-provider line because that is where a silent model change propagates from.

The adoption side, meanwhile, is accelerating on every measure a supervisor publishes. The EBA's December 2025 innovation monitoring puts EU bank AI adoption at roughly 90%, with general-purpose AI rising from near zero to about 65% of banks in two years (chart-derived figures). The Federal Reserve's April 2026 analysis of Census survey data puts US financial-sector adoption at 30% of firms — up 127% in a year — with 63% of financial-sector workers reporting work use of generative AI, the highest of any industry. The FSB's October 2025 report frames the systemic consequence: third-party concentration, correlated models (many firms wrong the same way at once), and — telling for anything built on monitoring — authorities themselves hampered by data gaps and no standard risk taxonomy.

The governance side is not keeping pace, and now there are numbers for the gap. IBM/Ponemon's 2025 breach study: 63% of organisations either have no AI governance policy or are still developing one. Only 17% have technical controls against staff pasting confidential data into public AI tools. Of organisations that suffered an AI-related security incident, 97% lacked proper AI access controls — and one in five breached organisations traces a breach to shadow AI, at $670K above the average breach cost.

In banking specifically, Wolters Kluwer's H1 2026 US Banking AI Risk and Governance Index (230 US banking professionals) found 72% named either model kill-switch protocols (34%) or regulatory reporting of AI failures (38%) as the area where their bank is least prepared — the two capabilities that only matter after deployment. (Correction from this briefing's first edition: that 72% is a combined either/or figure, not "72% lack kill switches"; an earlier "18% confident of passing review" statistic could not be traced to the underlying survey and has been withdrawn.)

Regulation has already moved to the same ground — with one twist that is itself evidence. The EU AI Act's high-risk regime, including credit scoring of natural persons by name, was due to go live on 2 August 2026. A month before that date, the Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, Official Journal 24 July 2026) postponed the Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027, leaving the Article 50 transparency duties in force on the original date. (Correction from this briefing's first edition, which stated the high-risk regime was already live.)

The point survives the postponement. The obligations are unchanged and lifecycle-shaped — post-market monitoring plans, serious-incident reporting, logged human oversight, conformity that must hold in operation — and the deferral moves the compliance deadline, not the risk: every mechanism in Part 2 keeps operating while the paperwork waits. The UK's SS1/23 and Canada's OSFI E-23 have run on lifecycle logic since 2023–24.

Singapore is the sharpest data point in this section, because it is the one supervisor that has moved from lifecycle language to a runtime specification for agentic AI by name. MAS consulted on AI risk-management guidelines covering all AI uses, agentic included, in November 2025. In March 2026 it published an AI Risk Management Toolkit built with a 24-institution industry consortium (Project MindForge — HSBC, Citi, UBS, DBS and others among them) covering traditional AI, generative AI, and agentic AI specifically. Then on 3 July 2026 it went further than any other supervisor in this briefing: Safeguards for Agentic Finance at Runtime ("SAFR") defines a governance checkpoint sitting between every agent's proposed action and its execution, so that no agentic action reaches execution without having been declared, authorised, and assessed — with the case for doing it at runtime, not at approval, stated in the paper's own words: it exists to enforce governance objectives at the moment an agent proposes to act, in production, at the speed and volume at which agents actually operate. That sentence is this briefing's thesis, written by a regulator rather than derived from a risk catalogue.

Part four

The path forward

The through-line of every source above — the MIT timing data, the two-quadrant split, the supervisors' concentration numbers, the banks' own kill-switch gap — is one shift: from approval as an event to appetite as a standing condition. Concretely, in seven steps. The sequence is practical, not a ranking of importance: the register comes first because every later step reads from it (expiry conditions flag register entries, pinning re-triggers register re-evaluations, sampled reviews and red-team cycles are scheduled off it), not because it matters most:

  1. Make the register the system of record, not the approval memo

    Every AI use case on one register with its verdict, the rule that decided it, the controls it depends on, and a full audit trail. If the register cannot answer "what is running, under which rules, approved by whom" in one query, nothing downstream works. This is also the raw material for the AI Act's documentation duties — and the only structural answer to shadow AI: a register cheap enough to use that going around it stops being the path of least resistance.

  2. Give every approval an expiry logic

    An approval should state the conditions under which it stops being valid: policy version changes, jurisdiction pack updates, vendor model substitution, control evidence going stale, re-attestation dates passing. Stale approvals must surface themselves — flagged on the register, not discovered in an audit.

  3. Pin models and detect substitution

    Version pinning plus output fingerprinting on vendor models, so silent drift becomes a detected event that triggers re-evaluation. The GPT-4 (50%→10%) and Anthropic (16% of requests) measurements make this the best-quantified post-deployment mechanism on record — and with top-3 provider concentration at 44% and doubling, a single silent update now moves many use cases at once. Zero tolerance for unmonitored vendor model change.

  4. Sample outcomes, not just designs

    Post-hoc sampled review of production decisions, scheduled off the register per use case — the control for the malfunction quadrant (225 risks): drift, disparate impact, degraded accuracy that no pre-launch test can see. For at-scale recommendation and decision systems this should be a standing control, not an incident response.

  5. Adversarially test in production conditions, on a cycle

    The control for the misuse quadrant (280 risks) — injection, extraction, manipulation — only testable against the deployed system, against an attacker capability curve that went 15%→93% in a year. Red-teaming belongs on a recurring calendar tied to the register, re-triggered by model or policy change, not done once at launch.

  6. Build the incident muscle before the regulator asks

    Kill-switch protocols, an internal AI-incident taxonomy, and reporting lines that can produce a serious-incident notification in AI Act timeframes. Wolters Kluwer's index makes this the sector's most concrete self-declared weakness (34% kill-switches, 38% failure reporting), and Stanford's recurrence data (3–5-incident firms up from 30% to 50%) says the firms that need it most are the ones deploying fastest.

  7. Feed what production teaches back into the rules

    A channel from operation to rulebook: challenges filed against rules, coverage gaps logged against an external taxonomy, precedent from decided cases informing the next decision. The evidence for needing it is in Part 1's own structure — the taxonomy runs to 24 sub-domains while most firms' rulebooks address far fewer, and the FSB's complaint that authorities lack shared risk taxonomies is the system-level version of the same gap: rules that never learn from operation. This is how a firm's appetite framework compounds instead of decaying — and over time it becomes the corpus no vendor can replicate.

The honest tension in this list. Step 6 — incident-reporting muscle — is the one the evidence in Part 3 argues for most directly: it is the industry's own self-declared weakest point (Wolters Kluwer: 34% named kill-switches, 38% named failure-reporting, as their bank's least-prepared area) and the most monitoring-native item on this list. It is also, by the same survey's own account, the one most institutions have not built. Naming that gap plainly is more useful than smoothing over it — a governance programme that is strong on paper everywhere except the one capability its own risk officers flag as weakest is not a governance programme with a rounding error, it has found its real priority.

The verdict, for a reader who runs a risk function

The evidence supports three plain conclusions. First, if your AI governance spends most of its effort before launch, it is aimed at roughly a sixth of the catalogued risk. Second, the two dominant post-deployment quadrants — deliberate misuse and quiet malfunction — need different standing controls, and most institutions currently operate neither on a cycle. Third, the regulatory deferral to December 2027 is breathing room for compliance paperwork, not for the risk itself; the mechanisms in Part 2 do not read the Official Journal. The practical move this quarter is step 6, on top of a register you keep current: know what is running, and be able to say what you would do within hours of it misbehaving.

How the numbers were computed

The MIT V4 workbook was parsed programmatically. The headline "84%" is 977 post-deployment ÷ (977 post + 190 pre) timing-coded risks. Of the repository's 1,724 total risks, 1,167 carry an unambiguous pre/post timing code, 430 are coded "Other" (ambiguous or spanning both phases), and the remaining 127 carry no timing code at all — the last two groups are excluded from the denominator, as stated beneath each figure. Quadrant counts apply the same rule to the entity and intent codes. Secondary research was conducted on 25 August 2026 by four parallel research passes (incident databases, regulators, industry surveys, empirical studies), each instructed to prefer primary sources and to flag what could not be verified; the verification ledger reflects those flags without smoothing. This briefing is analysis, not legal advice.
